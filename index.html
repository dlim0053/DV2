Australia's wetlands, and the birds that connect them to Malaysia

Wetlands clean water, soak up floods and shelter wildlife. Australia protects its most important ones under an international treaty, and some of the birds that depend on them stop over on Malaysian shores along the way.

Lim Ding Jian · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · 8 October 2026

Where Australia protects its land

A Ramsar site is a wetland listed under a global treaty because it matters internationally. Australia has 67. Protection is wider than that list, though. This map shows the share of each state's land inside any protected area, such as national parks, reserves and Indigenous Protected Areas.

Tasmania protects 42.8% of its land and the ACT 55.9%. Queensland and New South Wales protect about 9% and 10%.

What kinds of wetland there are

Start with the boxes. Each box is one wetland type and its size shows how often it appears in the Directory. The biggest are seasonal freshwater ponds and marshes (180), seasonal freshwater lakes (162) and freshwater swamp forest (155).

The rings of the second chart show how wetland types are made up. The inner ring is each state, the middle ring is the kind of wetland (coastal and marine, inland or human-made) and the outer ring is the individual type. About 57% of records are inland, 40% coastal and marine and 3% human-made. Queensland has the most records, followed by New South Wales.

A wetland is not one thing. The Directory of Important Wetlands sorts Australia's 851 nationally important wetlands into 40 types, from coral reefs and mangroves to inland lakes and farm dams. Darker squares mean more sites with that type in that state.

Tasmania stands out for coastal freshwater lagoons and marshes, found at 20 of its 89 sites.

Where the Ramsar sites are

Each circle is one Ramsar site. Size shows its area and colour shows the decade it was listed.

New South Wales has 13 sites and Victoria 12. The Northern Territory has two, yet they cover 2.2 million hectares, almost six times the area of all 13 New South Wales sites.

On the wing: a shared flyway

Migratory shorebirds travel the East Asian–Australasian Flyway each year. Its partnership network lists 26 key sites: 25 in Australia and one in Malaysia, Bako Buntal Bay in Sarawak.

The lines join that Malaysian site to the Australian ones. They show links in the network, not the paths of tracked birds.

Of the 37 shorebird species in the flyway's 2025 population estimates, 15 are on Australia's threatened species list: 2 critically endangered, 4 endangered and 9 vulnerable.

Species at risk

Australia's threatened species list holds 2,119 species that are not yet extinct. Plants make up 1,487 of them and animals the other 632.

Among animals, birds are the largest group, with 164 species. Seventy of them are endangered and 18 are critically endangered.

South Australia lists 85 threatened birds, more than any other state. New South Wales and Queensland list the most threatened animals overall, 263 and 245. A species listed in several states is counted in each. Invertebrates include insects, snails, crabs and spiders.

A treaty that keeps growing, slowly

The first Australian Ramsar site was listed in 1974. Fifty-one of the 67 sites were listed in the 1980s and 1990s, and only three have been added since 2010.

Queensland had no listed sites in 1990. By 2025 it had 640,000 hectares. New South Wales grew from 22,000 to 371,000 hectares.

Which sites, listed when, and how big

Of the 67 Ramsar sites, 29 were listed before 1990, 23 in the 1990s, 12 in the 2000s and 3 since 2010.

Ribbon width counts sites, not hectares, and that hides a big difference. Just 13 sites cover more than 100,000 hectares, yet they hold 89% of all listed area. At the other end, 35 sites are under 10,000 hectares, including all 10 of Tasmania's.
